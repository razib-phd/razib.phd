Research Profile

What I work on

I am an aquaculture and environmental researcher with over a decade of interdisciplinary research experience spanning aquaculture production systems, food safety and quality, environmental monitoring, and the socio‑economic and policy dimensions of aquatic food systems. My work investigates how aquaculture systems perform and are managed, how safe and nutritious their products are, how they interact with the surrounding environment, and how farmer behavior, markets, and regulation shape all of the above. I earned my PhD at the University of Copenhagen (Denmark), in collaboration with Patuakhali Science and Technology University (Bangladesh), building on earlier training at Bangladesh Agricultural University.

My research integrates molecular microbiology (qPCR, high‑throughput ARG/MGE profiling), analytical chemistry (GC‑MS, HPLC), environmental monitoring, quantitative and spatial analysis (GIS/remote sensing, statistical modeling), and large‑scale household‑survey methods to address production, safety, environmental, and economic challenges in aquatic food systems. I have worked within EU‑, USAID‑, and DANIDA‑funded projects across national and international consortia, trained field teams, mentored students, and published 18 peer‑reviewed papers. My current work spans environmental AMR surveillance, food safety and quality science, and the socio‑economic drivers of chemical‑input use in aquaculture — translating evidence into actionable monitoring, management, and policy approaches.

Project impact & achievements

Artemia4Bangladesh — WorldFish, EU‑funded

Contributed field research to a climate‑smart innovation initiative introducing integrated salt–Artemia production systems across Cox's Bazar, home to 95% of Bangladesh's crude salt output, produced by roughly 50,000 artisanal salt farmers on ~27,000 ha. The project turned idle post‑harvest salt pans into productive marine aquaculture, strengthening food and nutrition security for the half‑million people connected to the salt‑farming economy. Designed field surveys and sampling protocols and trained 20+ field staff in standardized data collection and quality control.

Harnessing Machine Learning for Aquaculture — WorldFish / BAU, USAID‑funded

Contributed to the first project of its kind to combine satellite remote sensing and machine learning (Google Earth Engine, Landsat, MNDWI, Random Forest, SVM) with statistically representative value‑chain surveys of 1,600+ farmers, traders, retailers, feed suppliers, and hatcheries to estimate aquaculture production and its contribution to employment, nutrition, and the regional economy of southwest Bangladesh. Work fed into public decision‑support data portals and online remote‑sensing/ML training delivered to partner teams.

Upgrading Pangas & Tilapia Value Chains (Bangfish) — DANIDA‑funded, led by University of Copenhagen with BAU and PSTU

Carried out PhD research within a ~10M DKK, multi‑country DANIDA consortium (2015–2020) spanning the University of Copenhagen, PSTU, BAU, NMBU, DTU, SDU, University of Göttingen, and Eurofish, aimed at upgrading Bangladesh's pangas and tilapia value chains for export‑quality standards. Investigated the microbial and chemical causes of yellow discoloration and off‑flavor in farmed fish using PCR, HPLC, and GC‑MS, generating evidence that informed farm‑management practices, water‑quality interventions, and value‑chain governance recommendations; mentored Master's students in experimental design and scientific writing.

Featured Research
Mapping microbial risk across Bangladesh's aquaculture regionsLead author
Malaysian Journal of Microbiology, 2021

Surveyed water quality across 38 pangasius and tilapia farms in five of Bangladesh's major aquaculture regions, using PCR to screen for fecal‑indicator bacteria, pathogenic Vibrio species, and cyanobacterial toxin genes. Pangasius farms carried significantly higher fecal‑coliform loads than tilapia farms, and Vibrio‑related genes were detected in three‑quarters of farms sampled — though not the specific genes tied to human or fish disease outbreaks. Genes for the cyanobacterial toxin microcystin were also more prevalent in pangasius systems.

Why it mattersOne of the first regional baselines of microbial water‑quality risk across Bangladesh's aquaculture belt, with a direct, actionable recommendation: routine, species‑aware water monitoring that farms and regulators could implement immediately.
A framework for deciding when the data will never be enoughCo‑first · corresponding
Ambio, 2026

Most environmental‑management tools assume that with enough monitoring, uncertainty eventually shrinks to something manageable. Using 35 years of wetland‑fisheries data from Bangladesh, this study shows that assumption often doesn't hold: disagreement between plausible models alone explained 40% of forecast uncertainty, with prediction ranges wider than anything seen historically. Rather than chasing false precision, the paper proposes a diagnostic framework that maps what's actually driving the uncertainty and prepares decision‑makers for a range of plausible futures instead of a single forecast.

Why it mattersBuilt to be transferable beyond wetland fisheries to any data‑limited, fast‑changing system — directly relevant to environmental and aquaculture management anywhere monitoring records are incomplete, which describes most of the Global South.
Why farmed fish taste different depending on where they growLead author
Fisheries Science, 2024

Compared the aroma‑compound profile of pangasius and tilapia raised in inland freshwater ponds versus coastal brackish‑water ponds, across two seasons. Found 35 distinct volatile compounds in the fish flesh, with concentrations shifting sharply by season and region — the widest swing was nearly 30‑fold in inland‑farmed tilapia. Coastal fish consistently carried fewer of these compounds, a pattern linked to tidal flushing diluting the agricultural run‑off and phytoplankton believed to be the source.

Why it mattersPoints to specific, manageable levers — site selection, water exchange — that could reduce the off‑flavor issues affecting consumer acceptance and export‑market competitiveness for farmed fish.
